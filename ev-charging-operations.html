Planning EV charging around fleet operations

Connecting vehicle readiness, site capacity, and energy cost to a feasible charging schedule.

An EV charging plan has to meet the vehicle’s next assignment within the power available at the site. Energy prices, other building loads, and local generation affect when charging is attractive. Arrival times, departure times, and battery requirements determine how much flexibility is actually available.

This paper presents a planning framework for managed charging, developed from EV-infrastructure use-case work. It connects business priorities to the data and decisions needed for a pilot. The capabilities described are proposals for evaluation in a focused pilot.

The charging decision

A charging site makes several related decisions: how much power to allocate to each charger, which vehicles need priority, and when charging should occur. Where on-site generation or stationary batteries exist, their availability creates further choices. These decisions need a common schedule rather than separate optimization targets that conflict.

For example, delaying every vehicle until a lower-price period can create a new simultaneous peak. Giving every connected vehicle its maximum charging rate may exceed the site’s available capacity. The operating objective must therefore include vehicle readiness and electrical limits alongside cost.

DOE guidance on managed charging distinguishes fixed schedules from systems that respond to changing vehicle and energy conditions. A static schedule can be a useful baseline. More complex control is justified when changing operations or site constraints create a measurable need.

The data behind a feasible schedule

Charger count, battery capacity, charging speed, and vehicle arrival and departure times are key inputs. These describe the physical opportunity to charge. A schedule also needs the energy required for the next duty period, rather than assuming every vehicle needs an identical charge.

Charging-session records should connect the vehicle, charger, timestamp, and delivered energy. The distinction between rated power and observed charging power matters: a rating alone does not tell the planner how much energy was delivered during a particular connection. Missing session records should remain identifiable when assessing the available history.

Site data adds the demand from other equipment, electrical capacity, energy prices, and any on-site generation or storage. DOE’s implementation guidance similarly brings together fleet inventory, operating schedules, utility rates, and building or grid data. The useful planning unit is a common time interval across these sources.

Where AI/ML can help

Forecasting and scheduling perform different jobs. AI and machine learning may help estimate uncertain inputs, such as charging demand under changing utilization or the generation available under different weather conditions. The scheduling step then uses those estimates together with explicit constraints to compare possible allocations.

This separation makes the result easier to evaluate. Forecast quality can be tested against observations. Schedule feasibility can be checked against departure requirements and site limits. A model that predicts demand accurately can still feed a poor schedule if the cost objective or operating constraints are wrong.

A useful first comparison is the existing operating practice against a straightforward rule-based schedule. A more advanced model should demonstrate additional value on the same representative periods. The development question is which decisions need prediction and which can already be handled reliably with known schedules and rules.

Where stationary storage exists, scheduling when and how much to charge it creates another decision. That introduces storage availability and operating limits into the problem. It should remain a separate scoped capability until the basic vehicle-charging schedule is understood. The presence of an EV or battery does not establish permission or equipment capability to export electricity.

A pilot that tests the decision

A focused pilot can replay historical operating periods before controlling chargers. It should include ordinary days and the difficult cases that affect service: late arrivals, an earlier departure, a charger unavailable for use, or less local generation than expected. These scenarios test whether a proposed schedule remains usable when the day changes.

  1. Define the service requirement. Specify which vehicles must be ready, by when, and what energy each requires.
  2. Establish the baseline. Reconstruct current charging behavior and the relevant cost and capacity measures.
  3. Compare candidate schedules. Evaluate readiness, peak demand, energy cost, and unresolved conflicts under the same assumptions.
  4. Review exceptions. Agree on operator priorities when the available time or power cannot meet every request.

Results from this exercise are planning estimates. Actual value depends on charger capabilities, communications, the applicable tariff, and how operators use the system. A later controlled trial should record delivered energy and vehicle readiness as well as the recommendation that was issued.

Making the schedule usable

Fleet staff own the departure requirements. Facilities engineers establish electrical limits and the interaction with other loads. Energy teams interpret tariffs and supply choices. Data and controls teams connect the inputs to equipment that can execute the schedule. These roles need agreement on priorities before automation is expanded.

The interface should make the next action clear: which vehicle is charging, its planned completion time, and whether an exception needs attention. An operator also needs a way to update a departure or override the plan when service needs change. This operational handoff connects the schedule to measurable cost and service outcomes.

A successful pilot provides evidence about feasible charging flexibility and the data needed to sustain it. It can inform infrastructure and operating choices without assuming that every site needs the same algorithm, storage system, or level of automation.
